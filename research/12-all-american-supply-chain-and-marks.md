### 1b. What the laboratories will print

- GIA Laboratory-Grown Diamond Report: no manufacturer, batch or country field; the trade press criticised exactly this gap against CIBJO guidance [S19]. GIA's Diamond Origin Report (country where mined) exists for natural diamonds only and only when the rough went through GIA Rough Analysis before polishing [S18].
- IGI Laboratory Grown Diamond Report: states the growth process (CVD or HPHT), "as grown with no indication of post-growth treatment" where applicable, type on request, and the laser inscription (LG plus nine digits) [S17]. IGI has printed grower and site statements in the comments (the WD example above). Whether IGI will add "grown in Wenatchee, Washington, USA" for a submitted lot today is a question for IGI **[verify]**; the 2017 report shows the mechanism exists.
- SCS-007 (Certified Sustainability Rated Diamonds): final version 1-0 released March 2024; covers "gemstone-quality diamonds, both natural and laboratory-grown"; origin traceability is one of its requirements and the 2024 update revised the traceability criteria [S20]. No US grower above was found on SCS's certified list **[verify]**; ALTR (India) and Creative Technologies publicise certification [snippet]. For the house, SCS-007 is a buyer's filter, not a claim to make.

### 1c. Natural diamonds: is any US production real

- Kelsey Lake, Colorado: the last commercial US diamond mine; closure dates in the record run from 1999 to 2002, with 2002 the most cited; closed over legal and financial problems, not exhaustion [S24][S25].
- Crater of Diamonds, Arkansas: worked commercially in the early 1900s, sub-economic; now a state park where visitors keep finds; "up to a few hundred carats" a year [S25][S26].
- Conclusion: there is no US-mined supply to buy. Americana natural diamonds are imported, as the provenance line already says. If origin ever becomes part of the Americana story, the honest route is a GIA Diamond Origin Report naming the mining country (Canada being the nearest), not a US claim **[verify as an option, not a plan]**.

### 1d. The documentation chain to keep (both lines)

1. Grower or dealer invoice naming the stone, weight, report number and, for a man-made diamond (laboratory-grown), the growth facility and city.
2. The report itself and the inscription number; verify once on igi.org or GIA Report Check and keep the screenshot with the invoice.
3. A lot register tying the house registry number to stone, invoice and report (this is also what the FTC's "look back far enough" standard needs, see 2d).
4. Natural diamonds: the revised WDC System of Warranties statement on every invoice and memo in both directions. Revised text, as published by DMCC: "The diamonds herein invoiced have been {sourced}* purchased from legitimate sources not involved in funding conflict, in compliance with United Nations Resolutions and corresponding national laws {where the invoice is generated}**. The seller hereby guarantees that these diamonds are conflict free and confirms adherence to the WDC SoW Guidelines." It applies whenever "a rough or polished diamond changes hands, both loose and set in jewellery" [S21]. WDC's page said the original statement would stop being valid on 21 September 2024 [snippet]; a July 2026 trade report describes the transition ending 21 September (2026) [snippet] **[verify the operative date with WDC]** [S22][S23].

## 2. US metals

### 2a. Gold: how much the United States mines, and where it goes

- USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (gold chapter): "In 2025, domestic gold mine production was estimated to be 160 tons; the value was estimated to be $17 billion." "Nevada was the leading gold-producing State, accounting for about 64% of total domestic production, followed by Alaska, which produced about 22%." Gold came from "more than 40 lode mines in 12 States"; "Commercial-grade gold was produced at approximately 16 refineries." Recycling: "an estimated 90 tons of new and old scrap was recycled, equivalent to about 60% of reported consumption." The same chapter notes "U.S. jewelry manufacturing was heavily concentrated in the New York, NY, and Providence, RI, areas" [S27].
- Mined doré goes to large bullion refiners, not to jewelry suppliers; Asahi Refining USA (West Valley City, Utah) is one such refiner in the public record, and USGS counted about 20 domestic refiners of commercial-grade gold in 2022 [S92][S95]; origin forensics (Metalor's "geoforensic passport") exist at the refinery level, and reporting on at least one US refinery describes domestic and foreign gold being melted together [S93][S94]. A mine-of-origin program selling karat grain to jewelers was not found **[verify]**.

- Can a refiner document that a specific lot is US-mined rather than recycled? Not among these five: all four refiners sell recycled metal and say so. "Recycled" is the documentable claim; "US-mined" is not, today.
- What the FTC requires to say "recycled": 16 CFR 260.13, already summarised in research/04 Part 3: content "from the waste stream," pre- or post-consumer, with the percentage qualified unless the whole product is recycled. A ring's metal can be "made from 100% recycled 18K gold (certified by SCS Global Services for [refiner])" only with the certificate on file; the diamonds and findings are outside that sentence.

### 2c. Platinum

- USGS 2026: "One company in Montana mined and processed PGMs"; 2025 US mine production an estimated 1,800 kg platinum and 6,200 kg palladium (2024: 3,010 and 10,200); production fell 40% "owing to one operation remaining on care-and-maintenance status"; net import reliance for platinum 89% [S28].
- Sibanye-Stillwater's FY2025 20-F: the Columbus, Montana facility "further upgrades the mined concentrates into a PGM-rich filter cake. The filter cake is then shipped to a third-party refiner for final refining" [S29]. There is therefore no US mine-to-grain chain a jeweler can buy into.
- What is obtainable: US-refined recycled 950 platinum grain (David H. Fell certificate names platinum; UPMR lists platinum grain; Hoover & Strong's Harmony line includes platinum per archived site text [snippet] **[verify current]**).

### 2d. Conclusion: "refined in the USA" is real; "US-mined" is not

- Realistic path: switch to one refiner, keep (a) the SCS certificate, (b) every lot invoice, (c) a one-page supplier certification letter. The FTC compliance guide: marketers need "competent and reliable evidence"; "if given in good faith, manufacturers and marketers can rely on information from suppliers," and a written certification of content "generally could" be relied on; marketers must "look back far enough in the manufacturing process to be reasonably sure" significant foreign content is counted, and "companies have an ongoing obligation to review their claims and substantiation" [S1].
- Sentences that become true at that point: "cast in [city], USA, from recycled 18K gold refined in Virginia" (or New York, California, Louisiana, naming the real refiner); never a sentence about where the gold was mined, because the refiner cannot say.
- The gold-ring example in the FTC guide ("If the gold in a gold ring is imported, an unqualified Made in USA claim for the ring is deceptive") turns on where the gold was mined or last substantially transformed. Recycled gold refined in the US is a stronger fact than imported grain, but the FTC has not published a position that recycled, US-refined gold counts as US content for an unqualified claim **[lawyer question, see 9]**.

## 5. Marking and the maker's mark

### 5a. What the statute requires once you stamp a fineness mark

- 15 U.S.C. 297(b): a manufacturer or dealer who applies a gold or silver quality mark to an article (or imports one bearing it) and moves it in commerce must "apply or cause to be applied to that article a trademark of such persons" that is registered or applied for within thirty days after the article enters commerce, or the person's name, applied "by the same means as that used in applying the quality mark or stamp appearing thereon," in type at least as large and placed as close as possible to it. Multi-part articles of differing quality must carry a mark of similar pattern and size on each part [S68][S99].
- 15 U.S.C. 295 tolerances: three parts per thousand below the stamped fineness for an article without solder; the whole article assayed "as one piece" may fall seven parts per thousand short "in the case of any other such article" (three for watchcases and flatware) [S68]. 16 CFR 23.3(d) incorporates these tolerances; its footnote repeats 3 and 7 parts per thousand [S69].
- 16 CFR 23.10: a quality mark is any letter, symbol or word suggesting precious metal; marks must be legible, placed where buyers see them and stay on until purchase; Note 2 restates the 297 trademark duty [S69].
- JVC, "The Essential Guide to the U.S. Trade in Gold and Silver Jewelry": "in the US, you are required to identify the fineness of the quality of the precious metal, but you do not need to stamp it on the item itself. You can identify it on the invoice, on a hangtag, or on other descriptive material." "If you do choose to quality stamp ... the law requires that you place a federally registered trademark in close proximity to the quality stamp." "The manufacturer's duly registered trademark is usually stamped on the product, or the retailer can apply a registered stamp." "Whoever's stamp is applied to the item will take responsibility and liability for the quality of the metal" [S70]. MJSA's compliance summary: violations can mean forfeiture, monetary penalties and jail, and competitors and trade associations may sue [S71].

### 5b. The marks themselves (16 CFR 23.3, 23.6, 23.7)

| Metal | Permitted mark | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 18K yellow or rose gold (750) | "18K" or "18Kt." (or "750" with the karat stated on the spec line) | § 23.3(b)(2): the karat designation must "immediately precede the word Gold" and be as conspicuous; "Gold" alone implies 24K [S69] |
| 14K | "14K" | Same; JVC: 10K is the US floor for the word gold [S70] |
| 950 platinum | "Plat." or "Pt." or "950Pt." or "Platinum" | § 23.6(c)(2): unqualified "Platinum" only at 950 parts per thousand or more; 850 and 900 must carry the number ("900Plat."); 500 to 950 with other PGMs needs each metal numbered ("600Pt.350Ir.") [S69] |
| White gold with rhodium | "14K" plus the disclosure "rhodium-plated" on the spec line | § 23.7: "It is unfair or deceptive to fail to disclose a surface-layer application of rhodium" [S69] |

### 5c. The practical path

1. File the maker's mark with the USPTO (word, design or composite) in Class 14 before the first stamped piece, or within 30 days after. Until registration issues, the statute still allows the application date or "the name of such person."
2. Make a steel stamp and a laser program for the same mark; apply it beside "18K" or "950Pt." at equal height.
3. Laser the house registry number inside the shank (Royal American: registry number plus Tennessee place name, per canon). The number ties the ring to the lot register in 1d and to the refiner lot in 2d.
4. State fineness also on the invoice and the spec line (JVC's alternative), so a worn stamp never leaves the piece undisclosed.

### 5d. Is "American Jewelry" registrable

- TMEP 1210.01(a): a mark is primarily geographically descriptive when "the primary significance of the mark is a generally known geographic location," "the goods or services originate in the place identified," and buyers would believe they do [S73]. The TTAB has treated AMERICAN as geographically descriptive and rejected the argument that this would make every use of it merely suggestive [S74]. "Jewelry" is the generic name of the goods. Expect a Section 2(e)(2) refusal of the word mark on the Principal Register.
- Routes: Section 2(f) acquired distinctiveness ("proof of substantially exclusive and continuous use ... for the five years before the date on which the claim of distinctiveness is made" may be accepted, but the Office "may, at its option, require additional evidence," and highly descriptive terms need more) [S73]; or the Supplemental Register meanwhile. The house has used the name for years in Tennessee **[years unknown, TODO]**, which is the 2(f) argument.
- Conflict check: AMERICAN JEWELRY AND LOAN, Reg. No. 3654202, standard characters, Class 36 (pawn brokerage), registered 2009 and renewed 2018 [snippet] [S75]. Different class, same words; a search for AMERICAN JEWELRY alone in Class 14 returned nothing here **[verify with a TSDR search by counsel]**.
- Design mark: a distinctive device (the star, once the paused logo is settled) is registrable on its own merits and is the better stamping mark, because 297(b) is satisfied by any trademark applied for or registered, and a device survives on a 2 mm shank where words do not. Interim: the legal name of the business as a name stamp satisfies 297(b) without registration.

### 5e. Exact wording the site's hallmark frame can carry

- Spec line, metal: "18K yellow gold (750)" or "950 platinum" or "14K white gold, rhodium-plated."
- Hallmark line, Royal American and Americana: "Marked inside: 18K, the house mark, registry No. 0231."
- Hallmark line, platinum: "Marked inside: 950Pt., the house mark, registry No. 0231."
- Hallmark line, New American: "Marked inside: 14K, the house mark, No. 0231."
- Under the frame, once true: "Fineness within 3 parts per thousand of the mark, as the National Stamping Act requires."
- Do not write "hallmarked": the US has no assay office; write "marked" or "stamped."

## 6. Metal standard decision

- What the houses publish. Cartier's own FAQ: "Gold (white, rose, yellow): 750/1000" and "Platinum: 950/1000," with the explanation that 18 karat is 75% gold [S76]. Tiffany's platinum page states that in 2022 "more than 98% of all precious metals sourced for jewelry, accessories and fine watches came from traceable recycled, secondary non-mined sources" [snippet] [S77]; its FY2020 10-K says directly sourced metals come from "in-ground deposits, primarily in the U.S." meeting its standards and from recycled sources [S77]; Tiffany pieces carry PT950 and 750 marks in the secondary market **[official materials page not fetched, verify]**. Van Cleef & Arpels: 18 karat (750) and PT 950 per third parties only **[verify]** [S78]. Harry Winston: product pages list platinum and 18K yellow gold **[care page not found, verify]** [S79].
- US mass market: 14K is the usual karat (retailer sources only; no primary industry survey found) **[verify]**.
- Trade-offs. 14K is generally harder and more wear-resistant than 18K because of the higher alloy fraction; the real figure depends on the alloy and heat treatment (a 14K patent describes solution annealing and age hardening to move Vickers hardness) [snippet]. Nickel white gold "is very durable and often very heat treatable" but higher nickel brings "a dramatically more brittle nature" and nearly always needs rhodium; palladium white gold is softer to work and may skip rhodium (Ballard, Ganoksin) [S80]. Rhodium wears and must be disclosed (§ 23.7). 950 platinum needs no plating, and the nickel question disappears (a jewelry guide notes nickel white gold "is not hypoallergenic") [snippet].
- FTC floor: "gold" alone means 24K; the karat must immediately precede the word; plated products need 10K minimum fineness; tolerances 3 and 7 parts per thousand [S69].
- Recommendation. Royal American and Americana: 18K yellow and rose (750) and 950 platinum; white only in platinum, so no rhodium clock and no disclosure line. New American: a decision for the family; 14K is the daily-wear and price choice, 18K is the house's color and a single standard across lines. State it once: "Metal: 18K yellow gold (750)." Never "solid gold" alone; never a plated piece at any line.

## 7. Does American-made sell

- Consumer Reports (2015): nearly 8 in 10 would rather buy American-made, and more than 60% said they would pay 10% more [S81]. Reshoring Institute (about 500 respondents, PDF uploaded 2020): nearly 70% prefer "Made in USA," 83% would pay up to 20% more [S82].
- 2025 data: Morning Consult's "Made in America" report (2025 edition): 59% seek out US-made products at least occasionally, down six points year on year; "only 2 in 5" will pay more; a companion report says most of those will not pay above 10% extra; women and younger consumers are the most price-constrained [S83]. Trace One (April 2025): 43% more interested in American-made than a year earlier, no group eager to pay a large premium [S85]. Conference Board: about half say US origin makes repurchase more likely, down from 61% in 2022 [S86]. Alliance for American Manufacturing poll (27 to 28 Oct 2025, n = 2,201): 76% prefer US-made "if possible," 82% over similar imports; no premium figure [S84].
- Jewelry and luxury specifically: the "10 to 60 percent premium" figure JCK repeated is the 2012 BCG study, which did not test jewelry [S87]; a state-made-goods survey ranked jewelry and accessories last of ten categories for premium willingness [S88]. No 2024 to 2026 jewelry-specific study found **[verify]**.
- The America 250 context: the FTC named July 2025 "Made in the USA" Month (1 July 2025) and sent warning letters on 8 July 2025 (four companies, plus Amazon and Walmart) [S89]; an April 2026 sweep followed a 13 March 2026 executive order, and July 2026 letters reached "manufactured in the USA," "built in the USA" and "#madeincali"; the FTC's stated premise is that consumers paying a premium for US origin "are entitled to" all-or-virtually-all goods [S89]. The agency is, in effect, telling the market that the premium is real enough to police.
- Honest read. The premium buys trust and a reason to choose, not a higher ticket. The people who care most are older, more conservative and already in the store; the people the remake courts (younger bridal buyers) say they care and then buy on price. A house should therefore say it the way Cartier says 750: as a fact on the spec line and the House page, with the refiner, the city and the stamp, and no flag.
