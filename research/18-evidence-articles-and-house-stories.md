## A. Evidence on first impressions, aesthetics and trust (and the rule each supports)

| Finding | Source | Rule it supports |
|---|---|---|
| A site is judged in about 50 ms, and that judgment persists. | Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006), "Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!", *Behaviour & Information Technology* 25(2) | The first screen is a photograph and a name, nothing else |
| Low visual complexity plus high prototypicality is rated most beautiful, within 17–50 ms. ✓ | Tuch, Presslaber, Stöcklin, Opwis & Bargas-Avila (2012), *IJHCS* 70(11) | Unique in photography, type and copy; conventional in structure |
| Perceived complexity and colorfulness predict first-impression aesthetics and can be computed from the page. | Reinecke, Yeh, Miratrix, Mardiko, Zhao, Liu & Gajos (2013), "Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics…", *CHI 2013* | One hue beyond ink and paper; one subject per screen |
| Visual aesthetics has four facets: simplicity, diversity, colorfulness, craftsmanship; measurable with a validated questionnaire (VisAWI; short form VisAWI-S, 4 items). | Moshagen & Thielsch (2010), "Facets of visual aesthetics", *IJHCS* 68(10) | §F: test the site with VisAWI-S before and after each pass |
| Aesthetics drives the first impression; content drives whether people return and recommend. | Thielsch, Blotenberg & Jaron (2014), "User evaluation of websites: From first impression to recommendation", *Interacting with Computers* 26(1) | Beauty on the first screen, substance (journal, specs, policies) underneath |
| "What is beautiful is usable": perceived aesthetics raises perceived usability. | Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar (2000), *Interacting with Computers* 13(2); also Hassenzahl (2004), *Human-Computer Interaction* 19(4) | The aesthetic-usability effect is why luxury sites get away with poor search; we do not need to |
| When judging credibility, people cite the design look more than any other factor (about 46 % of comments in a 2,684-person study **[verify]**). | Fogg, Soohoo, Danielson, Marable, Stanford & Tauber (2003), "How do users evaluate the credibility of Web sites?", *DUX 2003* (Stanford Web Credibility Project) | Design is a trust signal before copy is read |
| Online store design factors raise store image and the *expected quality of the merchandise*. | Oh, Fiorito, Cho & Hofacker (2008), *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* 15(4) | The site's quality is read as the jewelry's quality |
| Emotional usability and trust in interfaces come from specific visual factors (symmetry, color tone, simplicity). | Kim & Moon (1998), "Designing towards emotional usability in customer interfaces", *Interacting with Computers* 10(1) | Calm, symmetric, few colors |
| Color appeal affects trust and satisfaction, and varies by culture. | Cyr, Head & Larios (2010), *IJHCS* 68(1–2) | Paper and ink travel; test gold and navy with the local audience |
| Margins and white space around text improve reading comprehension and satisfaction **[verify direction of each effect]**. | Chaparro, Baker, Shaikh, Hull & Brady (2004), "Reading online text: A comparison of four white space layouts", *Usability News* 6(2) | 62ch measure, generous margins |
| Image quality measurably raises sales on a marketplace at scale (eBay data). | Di, Sundaresan, Piramuthu & Bhardwaj (2014), "Is a picture really worth a thousand words? On the role of images in e-commerce", *WSDM 2014* | The photography budget is a revenue line (research/14) |
| Serif credibility: in a 45,000-reader experiment, statements set in Baskerville were agreed with more than in other faces **[verify]**. | Errol Morris, "Hear, All Ye People; Hearken, O Earth", *The New York Times*, Aug 2012; Shaikh, Chaparro & Fox (2006), "Perception of fonts", *Usability News* 8(1) | Caslon for names and sentences |
| Five users find most usability problems; test early and often. | Nielsen (2000), "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users", NN/g | §F |
| Auto-forwarding carousels are ignored or resented; users rarely see slide two. | Nielsen (2013), "Auto-Forwarding Carousels and Accordions Annoy Users"; Pernice (2013), "Carousel Usability", NN/g | No carousels |
| Hidden (hamburger) navigation lowers discoverability and task success, especially on desktop. | Pernice & Budiu (2016), "Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics", NN/g | Five visible words in the header on desktop; Rolex can hide its nav, we cannot |
| About 57 % of viewing time is above the fold; attention decays down the page **[verify]**. | Fessenden (2018), "Scrolling and Attention", NN/g | What matters is in the first screen of every page |
| Anything that looks like an ad is skipped. | Pernice (2018), "Banner Blindness Revisited", NN/g | No promo bars, badges, chips |
| As page load goes from 1 s to 3 s, the probability of bounce rises 32 % **[verify]**; a 0.1 s gain moved retail conversion 8.4 % ✓. | Google/SOASTA (2017), "The State of Online Retail Performance"; Deloitte (2020), *Milliseconds Make Millions* | The 3D ring loads after the poster; budgets in research/14 §4.5 |

## B. Luxury marketing and status: the papers behind the anti-laws

| Finding | Source | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet goods are bought by wealthy, low-status-need buyers and priced higher. ✓ | Han, Nunes & Drèze (2010), *Journal of Marketing* 74(4) | The hallmark inside the shank; no logo prominence |
| Insiders prefer subtle signals that only other insiders read. | Berger & Ward (2010), "Subtle Signals of Inconspicuous Consumption", *Journal of Consumer Research* 37(4) | The registry number, the place name, the star |
| Luxury-branded clothing wins compliance, job recommendations and donations in field experiments: luxury works as a costly signal. | Nelissen & Meijers (2011), "Social benefits of luxury brands as costly signals of wealth and status", *Evolution and Human Behavior* 32(5) | The signal must be costly and true (the bench, the stone standard) |
| Labeling a luxury product "user-designed" lowers perceived quality and status; luxury buyers want the designer's authorship. | Fuchs, Prandelli, Schreier & Dahl (2013), "All That Is Users Might Not Be Gold: How Labeling Products as User Designed Backfires in the Context of Luxury Fashion Brands", *Journal of Marketing* 77(5) | **The builder is "Build on Signal", never "Design your own". The brother's design stays the author; the customer chooses within it.** |
| White space signals prestige and the meaning is shared by makers and readers. ✓ | Pracejus, Olsen & O'Guinn (2006), *JCR* 33(1) | `--section-y`, one subject per screen |
| Luxury online: the "internet dilemma" of exclusivity versus access, and how houses resolve it with control of presentation and service. | Okonkwo (2009), "Sustaining the luxury brand on the Internet", *Journal of Brand Management* 16(5–6); Hennigs, Wiedmann & Klarmann (2012), "Luxury brands in the digital age: exclusivity versus ubiquity", *Marketing Review St. Gallen* 29(1) | Show prices for Americana and New American; keep Royal American in the room |
| "Abundant rarity": luxury grows by rarity of the brand's meaning, not only of the object. | Kapferer (2012), "Abundant rarity: The key to luxury growth", *Business Horizons* 55(5) | Scale New American without diluting the house: same standard, same hands |
| The store manufactures aura through art and the service encounter shapes the customer's sense of status. | Dion & Arnould (2011), *Journal of Retailing* 87(4); Dion & Borraz (2017), "Managing Status: How Luxury Brands Shape Class Subjectivities in the Service Encounter", *Journal of Marketing* 81(5) | The appointment, the counter, the open bench are the product |
| A current review of luxury consumption psychology (motives, signaling, authenticity, sustainability). | Dubois, Jung & Ordabayeva (2021), "The psychology of luxury consumption", *Current Opinion in Psychology* 39 | A one-afternoon orientation for the family |
| Scarcity reliably increases perceived value. | Lynn (1991), "Scarcity effects on value: A quantitative review of the commodity theory literature", *Psychology & Marketing* 8(1) | One-of-one and set quantities, stated truthfully; never fake countdowns |
| Veblen effects formalized: some goods are wanted *because* they are expensive. | Bagwell & Bernheim (1996), "Veblen Effects in a Theory of Conspicuous Consumption", *American Economic Review* 86(3) | Do not discount; the price is part of the object |
| Round prices feel right for feeling-based purchases. ✓ | Wadhwa & Zhang (2015), *JCR* 41(5) | "$4,800" |
| Left-digit and precision effects; "$" signs and cents change perceived magnitude (restaurant checks were higher without the "$" **[verify]**). | Thomas & Morwitz (2005), *JCR* 32(1); Yang, Kimes & Sessarego (2009), *Cornell Hospitality Quarterly* 50(3); Coulter, Choi & Monroe (2012), "Comma N' cents in pricing", *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 22(3) | No cents, no commas-and-cents clutter; one price, once |

## D. How the houses built their myths (learn the method, not the costume)

- **De Beers, "A Diamond Is Forever" (1947).** Written by Frances Gerety at N.W. Ayer; named Advertising Age's slogan of the century (1999). Edward Jay Epstein's "Have You Ever Tried to Sell a Diamond?" (*The Atlantic*, February 1982) explains how the engagement-ring ritual itself was manufactured by advertising. *Lesson:* romance in jewelry is built, deliberately, over decades, with one sentence repeated. Read Epstein before writing a word about diamonds.
- **Patek Philippe, "Generations" (1996, Leagas Delaney):** "You never actually own a Patek Philippe. You merely look after it for the next generation." Still running. *Lesson:* one true idea (an heirloom), one photograph (a parent and a child), thirty years. Our version is the registry and the lifetime of care.
- **Tiffany & Co., the Blue Box and the Blue Book (1845):** a color so consistent it became a custom Pantone (1837 Blue) and a trademark. *Lesson:* a distinctive asset is a decision kept for a century (research/17 §2). Our paper and our navy must be used that stubbornly.
- **Cartier, Trinity (1924) and Love (1969, Aldo Cipullo):** a design with a story (three golds, three meanings; a bracelet closed with a screwdriver, meant to be put on by someone else). *Lesson:* a mechanism can be a love story. Our hallmark inside the shank and the engraving read live are mechanisms with meaning.
- **Van Cleef & Arpels, the Mystery Set (patented 1933) and Alhambra (1968):** a technical invention and a motif, each repeated for decades. *Lesson:* craft that can be named is craft that can be loved; "set and finished at our bench" needs its own named techniques over time.
- **Harry Winston, the Hope Diamond (1958):** he gave the world's most famous diamond to the Smithsonian and mailed it by registered post. *Lesson:* generosity and nerve make a house's legend; so does a story the press can retell.
- **Hermès, the windows (1978–2013):** four theatre sets a year around objects ✓ (research/17). *Lesson:* the brand is staged, seasonally, as a story.
- **David Webb and Oscar Heyman:** the American workshops that never left the building (research/13 §1). *Lesson:* the bench is the lineage we can honestly claim.

## E. Web-craft essays that shaped how good sites are made

- Oliver Reichenstein (iA), "Web Design is 95% Typography" (2006): the founding argument that a web page is set, not decorated.
- Frank Chimero, "The Web's Grain" (2015) and "What Screens Want" (2013): design with the medium's nature (fluidity, assembly, uncertainty), not against it.
- Ethan Marcotte, "Responsive Web Design" (*A List Apart*, 2010): the origin of fluid grids and art-directed breakpoints.
- Richard Rutter, *Web Typography* (2017): the most complete practical guide to setting type on the web, chapter by chapter from measure to OpenType features. Jason Santa Maria, *On Web Typography* (A Book Apart, 2014) and Tim Brown, *Flexible Typesetting* (A Book Apart, 2018) for the shorter paths.
- Ahmad Shadeed, *Defensive CSS* (defensivecss.dev) and his interactive articles: how to make layouts that do not break when content changes, which is most of "nothing shifts".
- Jeremy Keith, *Resilient Web Design* (free online): progressive enhancement as a design ethic; the reason the poster comes before the canvas.
- Heydon Pickering, *Inclusive Components*: the quality floor for menus, disclosures, tabs and dialogs.
- Rauno Freiberg, Emil Kowalski, Josh Comeau, Linear and Vercel pieces: research/10 §4.

## F. Tests you can run on your own customers

1. **VisAWI-S** (Moshagen & Thielsch): four questions, seven-point scale, free for non-commercial use from the authors' site **[verify current terms]**. Run it on ten customers in each store before and after each design pass; track the four facets.
2. **Five-second test:** show a screenshot for five seconds, then ask "what is this and what would you do next?" (Lyssna, formerly UsabilityHub, or in person on a tablet at the counter). The home page passes when eight of ten say "a jewelry house in Brentwood" and name an action.
3. **Five-user task test** (Nielsen): "find the price of a Signal band in yellow gold," "book a visit," "find what the warranty covers." Think-aloud, in the store, with a phone. Fix what three of five trip on.
4. **The hide-the-name test** (Romaniuk ✓): show an asset (the hallmark, the paper and Caslon, a registry line) without the name to category buyers; count who says American Jewelry. Twice a year.
5. **After launch:** Microsoft Clarity recordings and heatmaps (free); Shopify analytics; Google Business Profile actions; appointment and walk-in attribution (research/13 §5).
