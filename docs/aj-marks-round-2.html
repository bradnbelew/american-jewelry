Five marks, built to last

Each one is drawn as geometry, not typed, and proofed where a jeweler's mark actually lives: struck inside a band, foil-blocked on a box, and on a sign. The ones that survive the 1.5 mm test are the ones worth refining.

The Cipher

One glyph, two letters. The A's right side is drawn vertical so it becomes the J's stem; the hook falls away beneath and ends in a ball. High-contrast strokes in the Didone tradition, the same family of letter that Harry Winston and Dior build on. It reads as a single engraved initial, the way old houses signed their work.

Where it wins

  • Pure typography, no gimmick to date it
  • Engraves cleanly; the hairlines are the only risk at stamp size
  • Sits naturally inside a seal (see mark IV)

Watch for

  • Hairlines need thickening for the 1.5 mm punch version

The Lozenge

In France, every maker who touches precious metal must strike a lozenge-shaped mark into the piece: the poinçon de maître. Cartier's, Boucheron's and Van Cleef's are all lozenges. This borrows the form and plants an American star in it. Of the five, it is the most literally "jeweler's house."

Where it wins

  • The strongest heritage signal, instantly
  • Best of all five at the smallest sizes
  • A double rule and clipped points read as precision

Watch for

  • Letters inside need custom drawing; typed here
  • Must not get busy: the star is the only ornament

The Band

A single line weight throughout. The A is a prong setting holding a stone at its peak; the J drops from the right end of the crossbar and its hook is the ring's own shank, drawn as an arc that stops just short of closing. Read it one way and it is AJ. Read it the other and it is a ring in profile. It is the only one of the five that could be cast in gold as an object.

Where it wins

  • One stroke weight means it stamps perfectly
  • Becomes a charm, a clasp, a pendant
  • Modern without being cold

Watch for

  • Less "old house" than I, II or IV
  • The stone outline is the first thing to go when tiny

The Seal

The Cipher set inside a maker's roundel, with the house name and origin on the ring and two stars at the equator. This is the mark for wax seals, certificates, box lids and the shop window. It is not meant for the inside of a ring; there, the Cipher alone is struck. Together they work the way Cartier's script and its tiny stamped "Cartier" do: one for the world, one for the piece.

Where it wins

  • Packaging and paper feel official and old
  • Carries "Tennessee" without saying "local"
  • Pairs with I as a two-part system

Watch for

  • Only for large sizes, by design
  • Needs a founding year to be complete

The Ligature

Two letters tied by one bar. The A's crossbar runs straight through and becomes the J's, so the pair cannot be separated. The hook ends in a cut stone instead of a ball. Thin, even, geometric: the register of Celine, Loewe and the new wave of houses, and the one that will photograph best on a storefront and a phone screen.

Where it wins

  • Most contemporary of the five
  • Cuts cleanly from sheet metal for signage and clasps
  • Wide format suits site headers

Watch for

  • Thin strokes need a heavier cut for stamping
  • Least "American," most "fashion house"
Recommendation

A system, not a single logo

Cartier is a script for the world and a tiny stamped word inside the piece. Tiffany is a wordmark and a "T". The houses you named each run a pair. For American Jewelry I would run The Cipher (I) inside every piece and on paper, The Seal (IV) for boxes, wax and the storefront, and keep The Lozenge (II) as the fallback if the family wants a frame around the letters. III and V are strong directions for a more modern house; they belong on a shortlist for New American if that tier ever wants its own mark.

Pick two

One for inside the piece, one for the world. Tell me which and I'll draw three variations of each with proper custom letterforms.

Then the wordmark

"AMERICAN JEWELRY" set in a drawn serif to match, with the letterspacing and the Tennessee line locked.

Then the search

A USPTO and common-law trademark screen on the chosen mark before anything is stamped, printed or published.
