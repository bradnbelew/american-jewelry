American Jewelry · The playbook

How to build the house

The thinking behind the brand, the designs, the selling, the advertising and the transition, written as if I were running it. Every section ends in things to do. Nothing in here is a discount.

Part one

The idea we are selling

People do not buy fine jewelry because they need it. They buy a story they can wear and hand down, and they buy it from someone they trust to have told the truth. A house is the form that trust takes: a name, a hand, a set of designs that are recognizably its own, and a mark inside the piece that says who made it. Every house you admire sells exactly this. Cartier sells the Cartier hand. Van Cleef sells a clover it has drawn ten thousand ways. We are going to sell the American Jewelry hand, from Tennessee, and the proof will be inside every piece.

A house, not a store. Every piece begins as a drawing, is set and finished at our bench, and is marked for life.

Three claims hold the whole thing up, and each one has to be true before we say it out loud:

  1. Drawn here. Every design is your brother's. Nothing licensed, nothing from a vendor catalog. The drawing ships with the piece.
  2. Made here. Set and finished at our own bench, which a customer can watch through glass. (The legal line stays: diamonds and gold are imported; we say "designed in Tennessee, set and finished in our workshop," never "made in America.")
  3. Marked for life. The AJ hallmark and tier mark inside the shank, a numbered certificate, and the Lifetime Care Program. A house stands behind its name.
One honest caution on "the first American house." Tiffany has called itself America's house since 1837, and David Yurman and Harry Winston are American houses too. Say "first" only where it is true: the first house of fine jewelry in Tennessee, or the first American house built around made-to-order. "The American house, from Tennessee" is strong, true, and nobody can argue with it.
Part two

Recognition is not a logo. It is a small number of decisions repeated without exception until people learn them. Cartier's screw heads. Van Cleef's quatrefoil. Yurman's cable. Boucheron's four stacked bands. Each house owns one shape and one way of making, and then does them a thousand ways. These are ours.

Part three

What to design first

The mistake is to launch a whole catalog. Launch two motifs deep rather than six motifs shallow, and make one of them the icon. Every house has one piece that pays for the rest and teaches the public its shape: Love, Alhambra, Cable, the T. Ours should be The Star, in its simplest form, in solid gold, at a price a person can decide on in an afternoon. The Shooting Star signet is the piece that makes the house serious; the Star stud and pendant are the pieces that put the navy hexagon into a thousand drawers.

Make them unique on purpose

  • One shape, many pieces. The Star should exist at 4 mm, 7 mm, 11 mm and 16 mm, and only those, so a stud and a pendant and a signet obviously belong together.
  • Family stones as a system, not an afterthought. Every motif gets one designed place for a birthstone. That is the Family collection, and it is the reason a client comes back when a child is born.
  • The hidden star gallery under every center stone. Nobody else does it. It costs the bench ten minutes and gives us the thing people show their friends.
  • The tier marks inside: crown, star, line. A customer learns them in one visit and can read any piece we have ever made.
  • Numbered first editions. The first hundred of each design carry 001 to 100 on the certificate. After that, unnumbered. Scarcity without a sale.

Part five

How we sell

Price

  • Round, confident prices: $2,400, never $2,399. "From $" when the stone varies. "Price on request" for Royal American only.
  • Never a discount, a sale, a percent-off, a countdown or a coupon. The moment a house discounts, its customers learn to wait, and its best customers feel foolish. Houses add value instead: engraving included, resizing included, the box, the certificate, the care.
  • Financing exists and is never advertised. It is offered quietly at the counter and at checkout in the compliant words.
  • Promotions are releases: a new drawing, a numbered first edition, a Royal piece announced to the client list before anyone else, a trunk evening in the salon, a gift with a first purchase (the travel pouch). Scarcity and access, never price.
  • The upgrade promise, if the numbers hold: a New American solitaire earns a fixed credit toward an Americana piece within three years. It answers the one fear man-made buyers have and it moves people up the ladder on purpose.

The salon

  • Every conversation starts with the drawing. The advisor opens the drawing book before the case. "This is the piece. This is where it came from."
  • Every piece is handed over inside-up, so the first thing the customer sees is the mark.
  • The New American Standard card sits on the counter, printed, signed. We are the only store that publishes its line.
  • Appointments are the default for bridal, commissions and Royal viewings; walk-ins are welcome for everything else. A design code from the site is pulled up in ten seconds at either counter.
  • Repair customers, who are most of today's traffic, are the transition: every repair comes back cleaned, in a care card, with a look at what the bench is making. "While it was on the bench, we made this."
  • Titles matter: house advisors, the bench, the designer. Never "sales."
  • A client book: birthdays, anniversaries, children, the pieces they own and their birthstones. The Family collection sells itself from that book.

The transition

  • Vendor inventory leaves quietly: in-store, by trade-in toward house pieces, or through estate channels. No public clearance. A house does not announce that it used to be a store.
  • Repair and watch service stay as revenue and move under one name, the Workshop, and one program, Lifetime Care. The identity shifts; the cash flow does not stop.
  • Pre-owned Rolex becomes a discreet estate service with its own page and room, never in the house navigation.
  • The old site stays up and cross-links until the new one takes the domain; the 404s and missing location pages on it get fixed now, because they are losing search this week.
Part six

Advertising and channels

Luxury advertising has one rule: the photograph is the message. No prices in creative (except "from" for bridal), no urgency, no stock imagery, no emoji. Every piece of creative is one of five motions: the drawing becoming the piece; the bench setting a stone; the inside of a band; a piece on a hand in daylight; the navy hexagon opening.

The content engine

  • The Drawing (30 seconds): your brother drawing a piece, start to finish, no talking. Weekly.
  • From the Bench (15 to 60 seconds): one setting, one polish, one engraving. Twice a week.
  • Signed Inside (10 seconds): macro of the hallmark, the tier mark, the hidden star. Weekly.
  • Plainly (60 to 90 seconds): one honest answer about stones, grades, metals, care. Weekly. This is what AI search engines will quote.
  • The Registry (two minutes, YouTube): the story of each Royal piece. Monthly.
  • From the Bench, the email: one piece, one drawing, one sentence from the family. Weekly. Plus a printed client letter twice a year.

The designer is the face. People follow a hand, not a storefront. The family appears, named, in the House page and the letters. Nobody says "we're so excited."

Part seven

Market research worth doing

  • Mine the 2,537 reviews. Pull every one and read what people already praise: honesty, repair quality, staff by name. That is the brand's true reputation, and the house is built on it rather than against it.
  • The local set. Price and position every fine jeweler within thirty minutes (Green Hills, Franklin, Cool Springs, downtown) and the one house presence, Tiffany at Green Hills. The gap is obvious: Nashville has no house of its own.
  • Bridal volume. Tennessee marriages per year, Davidson and Williamson share, the Nashville wedding economy, and what share of engagement rings here are now man-made (nationally it passed half in 2024).
  • Search demand. The keyword map for Nashville and Middle Tennessee jewelry, bridal and custom terms; what the current site ranks for; where the 404s are bleeding.
  • Twenty clients. Sit with the twenty best clients, show them the three motifs and the Star, and ask what they would wear and what they would give. Design testing, done the old way.
  • Trademark screens on Royal American, Americana, New American, Shooting Star, Prism, Signal, Grid, and "American, by design," before any of it goes on a sign.
  • Price anchors. What a 1 ct natural solitaire, a man-made equivalent, a gold signet and a tennis bracelet cost at Brilliant Earth, VRAI, Blue Nile, Tiffany and the local set, so our prices read as a house, not a bargain and not a bluff.

Part nine

Everything I would change, in one list

Stop
  • Buying vendor jewelry for the cases.
  • Any sale, coupon, percent-off, or holiday discount.
  • Calling the business a store, a shop or "jewelers" in copy.
  • Leading with repair in the brand (keep the revenue; change the name).
  • Stock photography, emoji, exclamation marks, "we're so excited."
  • Letting the current site 404 on the pages Google sends people to.
Start
  • The eight codes, signed.
  • The Star as the icon; the Shooting Star signet as the hero; the Signal solitaire as the bridal door.
  • The hidden star gallery and the inside marks on every piece.
  • Numbered first editions and client-list-first releases.
  • The drawing book on the counter and the standard card beside it.
  • The weekly Drawing and Bench films, with your brother's hand in every one.
  • The client book and the Family collection as the reason to come back.
  • A printed house book, mailed, twice a year.
Make
  • The mark, the wordmark, the hallmark punches, the three tier marks.
  • The navy hexagon box, pouch, certificate folder, hangtag, bag, stationery.
  • The drawing cards and the certificate of making.
  • The salon ladder, the private room, the bench glass, the Star table.
  • The counter script, the compliance card, the care card.
  • A photography and film style guide so every image is ours.
Measure
  • Appointments per week, and how many came from the site.
  • Design codes created, saved pieces, bag adds.
  • Share of revenue from house designs versus vendor pieces, monthly, until it is all house.
  • Average ticket, and the Family collection's share of repeat purchases.
  • Client list size and the weekly email's replies, not its opens.
  • Reviews that mention the house, the bench, the drawing or the mark.
Part ten

What I would not do

  • Launch all three tiers with full catalogs on one day. Launch the Star and the signet; let the house grow in public.
  • Put the flag, an eagle or red-white-and-blue anywhere. American is a way of making, not a costume.
  • Advertise the man-made tier as the headline. It is the door most people walk through; it is not the sign over the door.
  • Chase influencers. Give pieces to three people in Nashville whose taste the city trusts, and let the drawing do the talking.
  • Claim "handmade," "made in America," "sustainable" or "first American house." Say the true thing, which is better anyway: drawn here, set and finished here, marked for life.

The whole strategy fits in one sentence, and it is the test for every decision from here on: would a house do this? If the answer is no, we don't.
